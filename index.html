Section 01 · Overview

Ojos Locos needs about 16% more weekly sales, and the files show where to look.

This is a first version of how CreditSwan could help Ojos Locos grow sales and EBITDA, for discussion with Comvest. It is built from the six files shared to date: the site-level operating model, management's November update, a board deck extract, two email threads and our first call, with public market data added for Texas and for rivals. Where a section needs data that has not yet been shared, the exhibit is marked illustrative and shows the shape of the analysis, not a result. Debt and lender matters are kept to one section of the Appendix.

Drawn from the files shared to dateIllustrative · placeholder values · not Ojos Locos data

A small fall in sales has removed a large part of store profit.

Ojos Locos runs 35 sports cantinas, 24 of them in Texas. Sales over the last twelve months were $158.0M, about 5% below a year earlier. Store profit, which is what the stores earn after rent and before corporate overhead, fell from $34.8M to $19.9M, because labor, rent and promotions did not fall with sales.

What holds. Sales are still about $158M a year, and the older Texas stores remain solidly profitable.

What to watch. After corporate overhead, adjusted EBITDA for the twelve months is $7.7M, against about $26M a year earlier.

Same-store sales turned negative in mid-2025 and have not recovered.

Same-store sales compare stores open at least 18 months with their own results a year earlier. They were positive through the first five periods of 2025, then fell to about minus 20% by early 2026. The World Cup lifted one period above zero, and the two periods after it were the weakest in the series.

What holds. The latest period, on management's update, is down 6.7%, the best reading of the year outside the World Cup.

What to watch. Comvest expects a weak holiday season, and there is no World Cup in 2027.

Sources: site-level model; management's update, slide 3; Comvest call. Assumption A-02.

Fewer visits and lower drink spend explain most of the sales decline.

Each store served about 10% fewer guests a week in the first ten periods of 2026 than a year earlier. Guests who came spent about 9% less on alcohol, while the food check rose about 10% after price increases. Comvest and management both point to immigration enforcement, inflation and the loss of pay-per-view fight nights as the main outside pressures. State tax filings show bars across Texas sold about 4% more alcohol this year while the Ojos Locos stores there sold about 13% less, so the market alone does not explain the decline.

What holds. Total food sales are up about 8% on last year, and lunch is growing.

What to watch. Guest counts span a change of point-of-sale system, so they need confirming from check-level data.

Sources: site-level model; Comvest call; management's update. Section 03 has the detail, and Section 04 sets the stores against the Texas market. Assumptions A-03, A-04, A-20.

Texas earns the profit, while California, South Texas and Nevada earn none.

Over the last twelve months the 35 open stores earned $19.7M of store profit between them. Houston and Dallas earned about two thirds of it, and eleven stores in California, South Texas and Nevada together lost about $0.6M.

What holds. The twelve strongest stores each keep 15% or more of sales as profit.

What to watch. Management's own list of loss-making stores has seven names.

Sources: site-level model, location summary; management's update, slide 10. Section 05 has the detail. Assumption A-05.

Management has cut cost this year, and its sales actions are still new.

Management says it has actioned about $4.3M of annual savings in 2026, most of it from security hours and corporate headcount, and expects about $1.0M more in 2027. On the sales side it raised alcohol prices in mid-October, launched two late-night offers on 26 October and 9 November, and is printing a new menu in December.

What holds. Lunch, with $10.99 entrees from Monday to Thursday, was up 16.7% in period 11.

What to watch. None of the sales actions has yet been measured against check-level data.

Sources: management's update, slides 2, 4 and 5. The Appendix lists each saving in Exhibit 9.05.

On management's figures, a 16% sales gain would restore most lost profit.

Management says about 64 cents of each extra sales dollar now reaches store profit, after this year's cost cuts. At $3.4M of weekly sales, against about $2.9M today, its figures give adjusted EBITDA of about $22M. Cost matters too: labor has risen from 22.9% to 26.4% of sales, and closing that gap alone is worth about $5.5M a year on today's sales.

What holds. The chain reached that level of weekly sales in spring 2025, with three fewer stores.

What to watch. The 64% figure is management's and has not been tested against store or check data.

Sources: management's update, slide 2; site-level model. Section 06 sizes each lever and has simulators for testing scenarios. Assumptions A-08, A-19.

CreditSwan proposes six workstreams, starting with read-only access to source systems.

The files show what happened at store level. They do not show why, because that needs check, labor and loyalty data. With read-only access to Toast and the systems around it, CreditSwan would tell Comvest which stores, hours and guests the decline comes from, and which actions are working, every week.

What holds. All 35 stores moved to one point-of-sale system this year, which makes access simpler.

What to watch. Timing depends on when management grants access. Section 08 lists the asks.

Sources: CreditSwan proposal; Comvest call. Section 07 has the plan and Section 08 the asks. Assumption A-16.

Section 03 · Diagnosis

Guests are coming less often and spending less on drinks.

This section reads the site-level operating model and management's November update to explain where the sales and the profit went. Every exhibit here is drawn from those two files. Guest and check figures come from period financial reports, which is as deep as the current files go.

Each store now serves about a tenth fewer guests than last year.

Across the first ten periods of 2026 the average store recorded about 1,870 guest checks a week, against about 2,080 a year earlier. In 2024 the figure was about 2,160. Total checks are almost flat, because three more stores are open.

What holds. The gap to last year narrowed to about 4% in period 10.

What to watch. The counts come from period reports that span the move to a new point-of-sale system, so the definition of a check may have changed.

Sources: site-level model, consolidated sheet. Assumption A-03.

Guests now spend less on drinks, while the food check has risen.

Alcohol spend per check has fallen from about $31 in 2024 to about $28 in 2026. The food check has moved the other way, from about $16 to about $18, after repeated price increases. Comvest's view is that years of price increases left the concept expensive for its guest.

What holds. The alcohol check recovered to about $30 during the two World Cup periods.

What to watch. Drinks carry a lower cost of goods than food, so a guest who drinks less is worth much less profit.

Sources: site-level model. Comvest's view from the call. Assumption A-04.

Alcohol has shrunk as a share of sales, which lowers margin.

Beer and liquor made up about 64% of sales in the first ten periods of 2025 and about 61% in 2026. Liquor sales are down about 15% in dollars and beer about 8%, while food is up about 8%. Because drinks cost less to serve than food, the shift takes gross margin down with it.

What holds. Gross margin is still about 77% of sales.

What to watch. Cost of goods has risen by about 1.6 points of sales in a year.

Sources: site-level model.

Late night is the weakest part of the day, and lunch is growing.

Management's update splits same-store sales by time of day. In period 11, lunch was up 16.7% and happy hour up 3.9% on last year, while dinner was down about 12% and late night down about 39%. Management links the late-night fall to immigration enforcement and has launched offers aimed at that part of the day.

What holds. Weekday lunch at $10.99 and weekend football are both drawing more guests than last year.

What to watch. Late night was already down by more than 20% before the summer, so the problem is not new.

Sources: management's update, slides 2 to 4. Assumption A-07.

The World Cup lifted sales briefly and then pulled demand forward.

Period 7 of 2026, which covered the World Cup, was the best period of the year at $14.3M of sales. The next two periods fell to about $10.9M each, about 12% below the same periods of 2025. Comvest's reading is that guests came more than usual during the tournament and then stayed away. Management points to high gas prices and back-to-school costs.

What holds. Sales have improved since, to $11.6M in period 10.

What to watch. There is no World Cup in 2027, so period 7 will be a hard comparison next year.

Sources: site-level model. Views from the Comvest call and management's update, slide 3.

Losing pay-per-view fight nights removed revenue that was almost pure profit.

Until January 2026 the stores charged a cover on big fight nights and priced drinks higher. When the fights moved to a streaming subscription, both stopped. Comvest puts the cost at about $3M to $4M of EBITDA this year. In the model, a small sales line that appears to hold cover charges fell from about $1.8M in 2025 to about $0.4M over the last twelve months.

What holds. Management is now positioning the stores as a value brand, without cover charges or event pricing.

What to watch. The $3M to $4M figure is Comvest's estimate, and the model line needs confirming with management.

Sources: Comvest call; site-level model; management's update, slide 4. Assumption A-15.

Store margin fell as labor and promotions stayed up while sales dropped.

Store margin fell from about 21% of sales in the first ten periods of 2025 to about 13% in 2026. Labor took the largest share of the fall, about 3.5 points, because labor cost per store stayed flat while sales per store fell about 13%. Promotions and comps nearly doubled as a share of sales, and cost of goods and rent each took more than a point.

What holds. Facility, repair and utility costs fell by about $1.6M.

What to watch. Labor is the line with most room, and it needs hourly data to manage.

Sources: site-level model. Assumption A-05.

Section 04 · Market and competitors

Texas bars sold more alcohol this year, while Ojos Locos sold less.

This section sets Ojos Locos against the outside world, using public sources only: state tax filings, company reports and industry trackers. The Texas Comptroller publishes every permit holder's monthly alcohol receipts, which gives a benchmark for the 24 Texas stores without any access from management. Three exhibits need data that is not yet in hand, and they are marked illustrative.

The Texas market grew this year, so the decline is not market-wide.

The Texas Comptroller publishes monthly alcohol receipts for every bar and restaurant with a mixed beverage permit. Across the state, receipts for January to September 2026 were about 4% above the same months of 2025. At the 24 Ojos Locos stores in Texas they were about 13% below, a gap of about 17 points.

What holds. June, the month the World Cup began, was the one month in which Ojos Locos beat the state.

What to watch. The match is by name and covers alcohol only, so it should be re-run by permit number before it is quoted outside this packet.

Other sports bars in Texas have held up far better.

The same source lets us look at other sports bars by name. Bombshells and Pluckers sold more alcohol in Texas this year than last, and Buffalo Wild Wings was about flat. Twin Peaks, whose parent filed for Chapter 11 in January, was down about 5%. Ojos Locos, at about minus 13%, sits below all of them.

What to watch. None of these chains serves quite the same guest, so a closer peer set should be built store by store.

Sources: Texas Comptroller open data; RCI Hospitality, Brinker, Dine Brands and Dave & Buster's releases; Twin Hospitality release of 26 Jan 2026. Assumptions A-20, A-21.

The Hispanic guest has pulled back across the industry since late 2024.

Industry trackers and consumer companies describe the same pressure that Comvest and management do. Black Box Intelligence found restaurant traffic in heavily Mexican-origin ZIP codes running about a point below the national average from late 2024. Constellation Brands, which says Hispanic consumers are about half of Modelo's drinkers, saw its beer volumes fall through 2025. Beer is about 41% of sales at Ojos Locos.

What holds. Constellation's volumes have been roughly flat since early 2026, which suggests the pullback has stopped deepening.

What to watch. A traffic gap of about one point in the trackers is far smaller than the decline at Ojos Locos.

Sources: Nation's Restaurant News and Restaurant Business on Black Box Intelligence; Constellation Brands releases; Brewbound. Assumption A-21.

A Houston sports-bar chain went from steep decline to growth this year.

Bombshells, owned by Houston-based RCI Hospitality, runs sports bars in the same Texas markets. Its same-store sales were down about 22% in the last quarter of 2025 and up about 12% in the third quarter of 2026. RCI credits a return to being a sports bar first, which moved the sales mix from about half beverages to nearly two thirds.

What holds. The Texas receipts show the same turn, from below last year in the first quarter to well above it since April.

What to watch. Bombshells has nine comparable stores, so it shows what is possible in these markets and is not a benchmark.

Sources: RCI Hospitality sales releases for fiscal 2026; Texas Comptroller open data. Assumptions A-20, A-21.

Whether Ojos Locos is priced above its rivals has not yet been tested.

Comvest's view is that years of price increases left the concept expensive for its guest. The files show the food check up about 10% in a year, and nothing on what rivals charge. The clearest outside example is Chili's, which says its guests spend $3 to $4 less per person than at competitors and has grown traffic on a $10.99 meal offer.

What holds. Ojos Locos already runs $10.99 lunch entrees, and lunch is its fastest-growing daypart.

What to watch. A fair comparison needs like-for-like items in each market, which is why the exhibit below is illustrative.

Sources: Comvest call; site-level model; Brinker International fourth-quarter fiscal 2026 summary. Assumption A-13.

Next year's sports calendar is thinner, and fight nights no longer bring cover charges.

The men's World Cup lifted periods 6 and 7 of 2026 and will not repeat. In 2027 the Gold Cup runs from 19 June to 11 July and the Women's World Cup from 24 June to 25 July, both smaller draws. UFC events moved from pay-per-view to a streaming subscription in 2026, which removed the reason to pay a cover charge at a bar.

What holds. Soccer still fills five weeks of next summer, and the Super Bowl falls on 14 February.

What to watch. Period 7 of 2026 is a comparison the stores are unlikely to match in 2027.

Sources: Fox Sports; Africa Soccer on the FIFA match calendar; Sports Brackets; Fox 26 Houston on the UFC and Paramount agreement; Comvest call.

Store-by-store benchmarks can start now, without access from management.

The state data is published by location, so each Texas store can be set against the bars within a few miles of it. That would show which stores are losing share locally and which sit in markets that are themselves falling. Foot-traffic data would give the same view for Arizona, California, Nevada and New Mexico, where no state receipts are published.

What holds. The Texas work needs no data from the company and could be done in the first two weeks.

What to watch. Foot-traffic data is licensed, so the second exhibit depends on a decision to buy it.

Sources: CreditSwan proposal; Texas Comptroller open data. Assumption A-13.

Section 05 · Store portfolio

A strong Texas core is carrying a weak tail of newer stores.

This section uses the site-level operating model to rank the 35 stores, and management's update and the board deck for the loss-making stores, the leases and the capital request. Store profit means four-wall EBITDA, which is each store's profit after rent and before corporate overhead.

A small group of stores earns most of the profit.

Over the last twelve months the ten most profitable stores earned about $13.4M, which is about two thirds of all store profit. Nine of the ten are in Texas and the other is Albuquerque. At the other end, six stores lost money on this basis and four more kept less than 5 cents of each sales dollar.

What holds. Albuquerque, Memorial and Dallas each earn more than $1.6M a year.

What to watch. Even the best stores have lost margin since the end of 2025.

Sources: site-level model, location summary. Assumption A-05.

Houston and Dallas carry the chain, and South Texas breaks even.

A Houston or Dallas store earns about $1.0M of store profit a year on average. Central Texas and Arizona stores earn about half of that or less. The five South Texas stores net to zero, and the five California stores lose about $0.1M each.

What holds. West Texas and New Mexico, three stores in all, earn about $1.2M each on average.

What to watch. South Texas stores take about $2.7M of sales each, about 60% of the chain average.

Sources: site-level model, location summary and site master.

The loss-making stores sit mostly in California and South Texas.

Management lists seven stores with losses in the year to period 10, totalling about $1.0M. Three are in California and two in South Texas, and the others are Tucson and Las Vegas. Management has held back November rent at five of them to bring the landlords to the table, and is studying whether the tenant entities can be put into bankruptcy to exit the leases.

What holds. Without the seven, management's figures show a store margin of 16.2% in place of 12.8%.

What to watch. Management's estimate of the gain leaves out legal, wind-down and guarantee costs.

Sources: management's update, slides 5 and 10. Assumption A-11.

Stores opened since 2021 earn far less each than the older stores.

The twelve stores opened before 2020 earn about $1.0M of store profit each. The 23 opened since 2021 earn about $350K each, and they include every loss-making store. Memorial, Pinnacle Pointe, Odessa and Mesquite show that a newer store can work in the right Texas market.

What holds. Those four newer stores each earn more than $1.2M a year.

What to watch. The capital spent on the newest stores is earning little, which bears on any request for more.

Sources: site-level model, site master and location summary.

Stores with higher sales keep a much larger share as profit.

Costs such as rent, managers and security do not shrink when sales do. Stores with more than $5.5M of sales keep about a quarter of it as store profit, while stores below $4M keep about 2%. This is why a modest gain in sales moves profit so far, and why the weakest stores are hard to fix by cutting cost alone.

What holds. Eight stores take more than $5.5M a year, and seven of them are among the ten most profitable.

What to watch. Twelve stores take less than $4M a year, and four of them lose money.

Sources: site-level model, location summary.

Every loss-making store has a lease running to 2033 or later.

The seven stores on management's list have leases that end between 2033 and 2043, with renewal options beyond. That is why management is looking at the tenant entities and their guarantors, and not at waiting the leases out. Three of the seven are guaranteed by one entity, two by another, and two show no guarantor in the lease schedule.

What holds. Eight profitable stores have leases that come up by 2030, which gives room to negotiate rent there.

What to watch. Management says its approach to all landlords, made with JLL, has produced very little movement so far.

Sources: site-level model, lease summary; management's update, slide 10. Assumption A-17.

Management has asked for about $6M of store investment that is not funded.

The second-quarter board deck sets out more than $6M of capital projects, led by a brand refresh and audio-visual upgrades. Six stores are named for the $2.25M brand refresh: four described as damaging the brand and two strong stores to protect. No funding for the work has been agreed.

What holds. Two of the six are strong stores with lease extensions under negotiation, where landlord money may share the cost.

What to watch. There is no return case in the files for any of the projects.

Sources: board deck extract, Q2 2026, slides 25 to 34.
